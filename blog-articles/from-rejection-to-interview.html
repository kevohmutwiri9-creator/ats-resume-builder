Resume Bullet Makeover: A Worked Example

A useful resume bullet gives a reader enough context to understand what you did and why it mattered. This fictional example shows one way to make a vague statement more informative without inventing achievements.

Important: The person and figures below are fictional for illustration. Do not copy them as your own. Use only details you can verify from your own work.

Starting point: a list of duties

Responsible for preparing a weekly operations report.

This sentence names a task, but leaves a recruiter with questions: What information did the report include? Who used it? What did the candidate contribute beyond routine preparation?

Step 1: Recover the context

Before rewriting, the candidate would check their own records and ask:

  • Which team or decision-maker used the report?
  • What sources or tools were involved?
  • Did I create the report, automate part of it, check its accuracy, or explain its findings?
  • Was there a measurable change that I can support with records?

In this fictional scenario, the candidate consolidates three spreadsheets into a single weekly report for a 12-person operations team. They add a validation step that catches duplicate order records before the report is shared.

Step 2: Choose details that are specific and supportable

The revised bullet should preserve the candidate's actual role, name useful context, and avoid claiming a business outcome that has not been measured. A possible fictional rewrite is:

Combined three weekly order spreadsheets into one operations report for a 12-person team and added a duplicate-record check before distribution.

This is more concrete because it identifies the work, scale, audience, and quality check. It does not claim the change saved a certain amount of time or reduced errors by a percentage; those results would need evidence.

If you have a verified result

If records support an outcome, add it with context. For example, a candidate might compare the number of duplicate records before and after the validation step over the same period. State the baseline, time frame, and how the result was measured. If you cannot verify a number, describe the improvement without adding one.

A reusable checklist

  1. Start with a clear action verb that matches what you personally did.
  2. Add the task, tool, audience, or scale that helps explain the work.
  3. Include a result only when you can substantiate it.
  4. Remove vague phrases such as “responsible for” when a direct verb is clearer.
  5. Read the bullet aloud and check that it remains understandable to someone outside your team.
